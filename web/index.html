01 / why a fly

A fly cannot be fooled by a pretty room.

the problemWorld models are becoming places to train and test robots. But the only check anyone runs on a generated world today is to watch the video and decide it looks fine. Looking fine and behaving right are different things, and no release note can tell you which one changed.
the instrumentOne reflex that has been grading approaching objects for 400 million years. The fly never identifies an object or estimates a distance. It reads light moving across a hex array of 223 facets, and when something is genuinely about to hit it, one neuron fires. Fire at the wrong moment and the fly is dead, so the circuit has no tolerance for motion that merely looks plausible.
the testWalk it down through eighteen levels and see where it breaks. Four calibration levels where we know the exact geometry. Eleven generated by Nano Banana, Veo and Reactor, with Backrooms entities coming down the hall. Three corrupted levels where we broke the physics on purpose, to find out whether the fly notices.
the verdictA level passes when the fly commits at the angle a real approach would produce. Not when the video looks good. On the seven levels where we know the truth, the fly commits at 20.7 ± 0.63°, against a published behavioural figure near 20°, and it flags two of the three corrupted levels with no false alarms on the four correct ones.

Escaping and passing are not the same thing. One level it escaped still failed, because it escaped far too late.

05 / the real world

What this is for.

World models are about to become the place robots are trained. Nobody can currently tell you whether an update to one changed how an agent behaves inside it. A fly can.

01
Robots trained in simulation stop failing in reality. A policy learned inside a world whose approaches arrive too late learns to brake too late. That is a crash in the real world and nothing in the generated video looks wrong. This catches it before the robot ships.
02
A world model gets a release gate. Point this at two builds and the difference is a number. V1 passes sixteen of eighteen levels, V2 passes eleven, and the regression has a name instead of being an argument about whether the footage looks better.
03
Self-driving sims get checked on the one thing that matters. Time-to-contact is the quantity an autonomous vehicle brakes on, and it is exactly the quantity this circuit measures. A driving world model that renders beautiful traffic with the wrong looming rate is a hazard, not an asset.
04
Evaluation gets cheap enough to run on every commit. 223 facets of numpy on a CPU, no judge model and no per-frame API bill. The alternative today is a person watching clips or a large vision model grading how convincing they look, which is the thing a generative model is already trained to win at.
05
The number is an animal's, not ours. A real fly commits near 20°. Ours commits at 20.7 ± 0.63° across an eight-fold speed sweep. A vendor and its customer can both check that figure against published literature, which is what makes it a benchmark rather than a dashboard.
06
And it does not stop at one sense. Approach geometry is one axis. A vestibular probe reads gravity, a tactile probe reads contact, insect optic flow reads odometry drift. Each is small, cheap, and has a correct answer that was settled long before the internet. Together they are a physics test suite for generated reality.

We did not build a better world model. We built the part that tells you when one is lying.
